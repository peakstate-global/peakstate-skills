## Layer 2: Systemic causes

**Thread 1: working-class and union.**
- Drivers: productivity has risen while standard hours have stayed near 38 for decades (RECALLED, dates `[to confirm]`); enterprise bargaining is the main channel for hours change; union density has fallen, so fewer workplaces can bargain for it; award rules on ordinary hours and overtime set the shape of the week.
- Incentives that keep it in place: employers pay overtime and penalty rates rather than hire, since hiring carries fixed costs; workers on low pay prefer paid hours to time off.

**Thread 2: libertarian, individual bargain.**
- Drivers: the right to request flexible work under the Fair Work Act was strengthened in 2023 (RECALLED); a tight labour market lets skilled workers negotiate; remote work made compressed weeks easier to hide or trade.
- Incentives: firms offer the four-day week as a retention perk instead of a pay rise; individual deals avoid a precedent that would bind the whole workforce.

**Thread 3: Buddhist, contemplative.**
- Drivers: spare time fills with consumption and screens; the attention economy monetises the extra day; burnout is treated as an individual problem, handled by leave and apps.
- Incentives: platforms earn from attention, not rest; a person gets status from being busy.

**Thread 4: First Nations, relational.**
- Drivers: leave categories assume a nuclear family and a Christian calendar; cultural and ceremonial obligations fit poorly in annual leave; First Nations workers carry obligations to community that employers do not see.
- Incentives: standard leave is simpler to administer; employers are rarely asked to count community work as real work.

**Usual fix at this layer:** legislate or bargain it. Change the awards, fund a public-sector trial, add a right to request a compressed week.

Sideways diagnoses from contrasting lenses:

- **Feminist, care economy:** the week is designed around a worker with someone at home. The fifth day lands on whoever does the unpaid care, so a four-day week can widen the gap unless care is shared.
- **Small-business owner, individual:** the owner is the worker of last resort. When staff drop a day, the owner works it, so the four-day week moves hours up to the one person with no award.
- **Jewish, Sabbath tradition:** rest is a commandment held in common, not a benefit negotiated one worker at a time. A rest day that every person takes on a different day is weaker than one taken together.
- **Global South, labour chain:** Australian firms can cut to four days partly because customer service, coding and back-office work run offshore at longer hours. The fifth day may be worked in Manila or Bengaluru.
- **Ecological:** fewer commuting days cut transport emissions, but the saving can be spent on travel and consumption.
- **Technocratic, fiscal:** shorter hours reduce tax receipts per worker unless output per hour rises, and an ageing population makes that harder.

## Layer 3: Worldview

**Thread 1: feminist, care economy.** The paradigm under the usual debate is the male-breadwinner model: work is what is paid, and care happens off the books. It centres the salaried full-time worker. It leaves out carers, most of them women, and the people they care for. It treats the five-day week as universal, when it is an industrial-era arrangement that assumed someone else at home.

**Thread 2: small-business owner.** The paradigm is the owner as self-made individual who carries the risk and earns the reward. It centres the owner and the customer. It leaves out the staff, whose hours are a cost line. It treats "always open" as universal, when it is a recent retail habit.

**Thread 3: Jewish, Sabbath tradition.** The dominant paradigm here is secular time: every hour is interchangeable and can be sold. It centres the market calendar. It leaves out religious calendars of all kinds. It treats the 24/7 week as neutral, when it is one culture's choice.

**Thread 4: Global South, labour chain.** The paradigm is national accounting: Australian productivity is measured inside Australian borders. It centres the Australian worker and shareholder. It leaves out the offshore worker who absorbs the hours. It treats "our" productivity as local, when part of it is imported.

Alternative worldviews:

- **Catholic social teaching (religious, collective):** work serves the person, not the reverse, and a just wage includes time for family and worship. The future is for a family and a community that can gather, and the economy exists to make that possible.
- **Liberal self-ownership (individual, autonomy):** my time is my property, and I may sell as much or as little as I choose. The future is for maximum choice, so the goal is to remove the rules that lock people into a five-day week, not to impose a four-day one.
- **Premodern agrarian and feast-day calendar (premodern):** before the industrial clock, the year ran on seasons and holy days, and many medieval calendars had dozens of feast days off (RECALLED, count `[to confirm]`). The future is for a rhythm set by season and festival, not a flat count of days.
- **Post-work automation dividend (postmodern, emerging):** if machines and AI do more of the work, hours should fall as a shared dividend. The future is for deciding how to spend the time the machines free, and the fight is over who gets the dividend.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander relational worldview (non-Western, collective):** a person is defined by relationships to kin and Country, and obligations to both come before a wage. The future is for keeping those relationships alive across generations.
- **Confucian household (civilisational, collective):** diligence is a family duty and hard work honours parents and children. The future is for the continuity and standing of the family, so shorter hours read as a risk, not a reward.
- **Degrowth ecological (emerging, collective):** less paid work means less production and less consumption, which is the point. The future is for living well within the planet's limits.

**Checkpoint C, auto picks** (all four lenses change from Layer 2):

- **Catholic social teaching**: religious axis, and collective.
- **Liberal self-ownership**: individual autonomy axis.
- **Premodern agrarian and feast-day calendar**: non-modern axis.
- **Post-work automation dividend**: emerging axis.

## Layer 5: Build back up

### Stack A: the fallow field

- **Metaphor:** a field left unsown for a season so it can bear again. Rest is maintenance of capacity, not a reward for output.
- **Worldview:** people, like land, lose capacity if worked every season. Rest is scheduled in advance as part of the plan, not earned after the harvest.
- **Systemic levers:**
  - Policy: make long service leave portable across employers in more industries, so the long fallow is not lost when a worker changes jobs (portable schemes already exist in some industries, RECALLED, list `[to confirm]`).
  - Policy: a statutory sabbatical option every seven years, funded by salary sacrifice.
  - Organisational: rotate the day off across teams, so the business stays open while every person rests, as crops rotate across fields.
  - Organisational: plan capacity at 80% of hours, and put the slack in the plan, not in overtime.
  - Personal: set one fixed fallow day a week with no work email, and protect it before booking anything else.
- **New litany:** "Rotating rest day becomes the norm in hospitals and councils"; "Portable long service leave extended to all industries"; "Burnout claims fall for the third year running". **New metrics:** share of workers with one full work-free day each week; share of workers who took a leave block of four weeks or more in the last seven years.
- **First moves this quarter:** map which days each person in your team is actually off; trial a rotating fifth day for one team; check whether your industry has a portable long service leave scheme.

### Stack B: the breath

- **Metaphor:** each person breathes at their own rate. A good working week fits the person's rhythm, not one rhythm for all.
- **Worldview:** time is personal property, and the aim is to let each worker choose the shape of their own week, whether that is four long days, five short ones, or nine days a fortnight.
- **Systemic levers:**
  - Policy: widen the right to request so a compressed or reduced week has to be granted unless the employer shows a specific business reason.
  - Policy: keep the right to disconnect, which took effect from August 2024 for larger employers (RECALLED), and extend it to small business.
  - Organisational: publish a menu of week shapes, and let people move between them each quarter.
  - Organisational: judge work by agreed outcomes, not hours seen.
  - Personal: track your own energy across a month and pick the week shape that fits it.
- **New litany:** "Half of workers now choose their own week shape"; "Nine-day fortnight overtakes the five-day week in the public service". **New metric:** the gap between hours people work and the hours they say they would prefer to work.
- **First moves this quarter:** ask each person in your team what week shape they would choose; offer one alternative shape to anyone who asks; log your own energy by day for four weeks.

### Stack C: the Sabbath

- **Metaphor:** one day when everyone stops together. The value is the shared stop, not the private day off.
- **Worldview:** rest is held in common. A day off that each person takes alone leaves families and communities unable to meet, so the shared day matters more than the count of days.
- **Systemic levers:**
  - Policy: keep penalty rates for work on the shared rest day, so stopping together stays the default.
  - Policy: coordinate a common day off across the public sector in a region, so schools, services and families can plan around it.
  - Organisational: close the whole organisation on the fifth day, rather than rotate, and make that visible to customers.
  - Community: clubs, churches, mosques, temples and councils schedule gatherings on the shared day.
  - Personal: agree one shared day with the people you live with, and keep it free of paid work.
- **New litany:** "Region adopts common Friday off; sport and volunteering rise"; "Families report more shared meals". **New metric:** share of households where all working adults share the same day off.
- **First moves this quarter:** find which day your household or team could stop together; ask one community group what it would run if people had that day free.

### Stack D: Country time

- **Metaphor:** time belongs to relationships with kin and place. The day not worked for wages is a day of obligation and care.
- **Worldview:** a person is defined by relationships to kin and Country, and the working week should make room for those obligations. This stack must be shaped and led by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, not borrowed by others.
- **Systemic levers:**
  - Policy: include cultural and ceremonial leave in awards and agreements (some public-sector agreements already include it, RECALLED, detail `[to confirm]`).
  - Policy: count caring for Country and community, such as ranger work, as recognised work in public accounts.
  - Organisational: co-design leave and the week shape with First Nations staff, and let obligations, not a fixed calendar, set the non-work day.
  - Organisational: measure contribution to community alongside output.
  - Personal: ask what you owe to the place you live and the people around you, and spend part of the extra day on it.
- **New litany:** "Cultural leave now standard in national awards"; "Employers report fewer resignations among First Nations staff". **New metric:** hours per week spent in care of kin, community and place.
- **First moves this quarter:** check what cultural and ceremonial leave your organisation offers; ask First Nations staff or a local Aboriginal organisation what the week gets wrong, and listen before proposing anything.

## Reflection
All four stacks agree that the productivity machine is the wrong test: none of them judges the four-day week only by whether output holds.
They split on one axis above all: whether rest is private (the breath) or shared (the Sabbath, and Country time).
The breath is the easiest to adopt, because it fits the current bargaining system, and it is the one most likely to leave carers and shift workers behind.
The Sabbath and the fallow field need coordination across firms, so they depend on unions, awards or government.
The split matters because "on whose terms" in the question is decided less by the number of days than by who chooses which day.

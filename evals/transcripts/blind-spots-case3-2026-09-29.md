### Possible biases and fallacies

**False dilemma (fallacy), high confidence**
- Passage: "Either we protect our businesses or we hand the street to cyclists."
- Generous reading: she may mean that road space is fixed, so every metre given to a lane is a metre taken from parking or loading that shops use. That would be sound if the design truly left no room for both.
- Concern: this sentence may present two options when others exist, such as a lane that keeps loading zones, a trial period, or a different street for the lane.
- Checking question: What third option has not been named, and was a design that keeps parking or loading considered?

**False cause (fallacy), medium confidence**
- Passage: "Since the Smith Road lane opened, two cafes on Smith Road have closed."
- Generous reading: she may know of a direct link, for example the lane removed the parking those cafes relied on. With that link shown and other causes ruled out, the claim would be sound.
- Concern: this sentence may treat one thing following another as proof that the first caused the second.
- Checking question: What else changed for those two cafes in the same period?

**Hasty generalisation (fallacy), medium confidence**
- Passage: "Bike lanes on Main Street will kill local shops." read with "two cafes on Smith Road have closed."
- Generous reading: two closures on a short street can be a large share of its shops, and she may be pointing to an early warning rather than a proven rule.
- Concern: the passage may draw a firm prediction about all Main Street shops from two cases on one other street.
- Checking question: How many shops are on Smith Road, how many opened or stayed open after the lane, and are these two cafes typical of them?

**Appeal to authority (fallacy), medium confidence**
- Passage: "Every serious planner knows lanes hurt trade."
- Generous reading: she may have read planning advice or reports that warn about trade effects. If she can name them, and planners in the field do not dispute them, the appeal would be sound.
- Concern: this sentence may put an unnamed group of experts in place of evidence.
- Checking question: Which planners or reports, and do other planners in the field agree?

**No true Scotsman (fallacy), low confidence**
- Passage: "Every serious planner"
- Generous reading: "serious" may only mean experienced or qualified, fixed before anyone disagreed.
- Concern: the word "serious" may rule out any planner who disagrees, so the claim cannot be tested by a counter-example.
- Checking question: Would a qualified planner who found that lanes helped trade count as serious?

On "Shop owners I have spoken to are worried": I have not listed this as a fallacy. Their worry is fair input for a council to hear. It becomes a weak point only if it is offered as evidence of what will happen, which is assumption A5 above.

## Layer 2: Systemic causes

### Thread 1: Ghost towers

**Drivers (STEEP):**

- **Technological:** video calls, cloud tools and shared documents make most desk work possible from anywhere, so the office is no longer the only place the work can happen.
- **Social:** workers built home routines during the lockdowns, and many now weigh the commute (often an hour or more a day, RECALLED `[to confirm]`) against what they get in the office.
- **Economic:** office leases run for five to fifteen years, so empty space shows up in vacancy figures slowly, as leases end and firms take less floor space.
- **Economic:** older, lower-grade buildings lose tenants first, because firms that do come in want the newest, best-located floors ("flight to quality").
- **Political:** city-centre zoning, building codes and floor plates were set for single-use office districts, which makes conversion to homes slow and costly.
- **Environmental:** newer buildings meet energy and emissions standards that older stock cannot meet without deep, costly refits.

**Incentives that keep it in place:**

- Owners and lenders can avoid writing down a building's value while it is still "let" on paper, so empty space stays on the books at old prices and is not repriced or repurposed.
- Firms save rent by shrinking floor space, and each firm gains from cutting its own space even if the centre as a whole loses foot traffic.
- Councils rely on property rates and business levies from the centre, so they defend the office district rather than rezone it.

**Usual fix at this layer:** office-to-residential conversion schemes, tax relief for refits, rezoning for mixed use, and investment in transport and public space to make the centre worth the trip.

### Thread 2: Return-to-office mandates

**Drivers (STEEP):**

- **Social:** managers learned to lead by seeing people, and many do not trust output they cannot see.
- **Economic:** firms hold long leases on space they are paying for, so empty desks look like waste to the board.
- **Economic:** when the labour market is loose, employers have more power to set terms; when it is tight, workers do (RECALLED as a general pattern `[to confirm]` for any given city).
- **Technological:** remote tools handle planned work well but handle chance meetings, mentoring and tacit learning less well, and leaders point to this.
- **Political:** some governments and councils publicly back office returns to support city-centre businesses and transport revenue.

**Incentives that keep it in place:**

- Senior leaders built their careers in the office, so visible presence is how they learned to judge commitment and how they were promoted.
- A mandate can reduce headcount quietly, because some staff leave rather than comply, and no redundancy is paid.
- Landlords, city businesses and transport operators all gain from mandates and lobby for them.

**Usual fix at this layer:** hybrid policies with set "anchor days", badge-swipe tracking, redesigned offices with more collaboration space, and output-based performance measures.

### Sideways diagnoses (contrasting lenses)

- **Libertarian (individual):** the problem is not remote work but mandates and planning rules. Let owners convert, sell or demolish freely and let each firm and worker strike their own deal, and the centre will find its own use.
- **Working-class or craft (collective):** the diagnosis leaves out the people whose work cannot move. Cleaners, security guards, café staff and transport workers lose hours when the towers empty, and their losses do not appear in office vacancy figures.
- **Feminist:** mandates fall hardest on carers, most of them women, because the flexible hours that let them hold paid work disappear. The cause is how care is organised, not where the laptop sits.
- **Islamic (civilisational):** a city centre has long been organised around shared places of prayer, market and gathering, so the question is why the modern centre was built around a single use, and what obligations a community owes to its common spaces.
- **Confucian (collective):** firms and teams are relationships of duty between senior and junior, and remote work weakens the mentoring and face-to-face obligation that hold them together. The mandate is a clumsy attempt to repair that.
- **Ecological:** the systemic cause is a property model that treats buildings as disposable assets. Empty towers hold embodied carbon, and demolishing them to rebuild is the worst outcome.
- **Global South:** the same tools that empty the Western tower move work to offshore centres, so one city's vacancy is another city's growth, and the "city centre problem" is a global labour-market shift.
- **Technocratic:** the problem is poor data. Cities and firms lack good measures of attendance, productivity and foot traffic, so they argue from anecdote and set policy by headline.

## Layer 2: Systemic causes, second set

- **Conservative (civic tradition, collective):** the centre lost its civic institutions long before remote work. Churches, clubs, department stores, civic halls and markets gave way to single-use office blocks, so when the office workers stayed home there was little else holding the centre together. Remote work exposed a hollow core; it did not create one.
- **Populist:** the centre was built and subsidised for a professional class. Transport spending, events and public space flow to the CBD, while outer suburbs and regional towns carry the long commutes and get less. The "decline of the city centre" is how that class describes its own loss, and much of the population may not share the concern.
- **Statist (infrastructure lock-in):** radial rail and road networks were built to pour workers into one point at peak hours, and transport budgets and fares depend on those peak trips. The network, not the office, is what locks the pattern in place. While the network stays radial, every fix tries to refill the old centre rather than serve new places.
- **Decolonial:** the CBD grid was laid over land that was already a place of meeting and exchange, and it turned that land into a financial asset. The systemic cause is land treated mainly as a store of value, so an empty tower is a problem for the balance sheet first and for the community second.
- **Jewish (the rhythm of the week):** the old city ran on a shared weekly rhythm of five days in, two days off, and every business in the centre was built around it. Hybrid work broke the shared week into private schedules, which is why Mondays and Fridays are empty. The missing piece is an institution that sets a new common rhythm, not a mandate that restores the old one.
- **Intergenerational:** the costs land on people not in the room when the decisions are made. Young workers lose the apprentice years they would have spent learning beside others, and the next generation inherits stranded buildings, their debt and their embodied carbon.
- **Individual (the worker as free agent):** the employment contract itself is loosening. Remote work is one part of a longer shift in which skilled workers act more like firms of one, choosing where, when and for whom they work. On this reading the office district is losing a monopoly on where work is sold.
- **Non-human:** the system treats the centre as a place for people and money only. Quieter streets and empty roofs could be habitat, water catchment and cooling for a hotter city, and nothing in the current incentives counts those uses.

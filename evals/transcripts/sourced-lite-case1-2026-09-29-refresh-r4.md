## Final position

The evidence supports fewer management layers and wider spans of control, and it does not support "most middle-management roles will go" as a general prediction. Even the boldest forecast found expects deep cuts in only one in five organisations (C1), and the people part of the role (feedback, engagement, development) still drives results (C6, C7). The claim holds in organisations where manager roles are mostly relay and reporting work, and fails where the manager's main job is developing and directing people (C11, C12).

## Adversarial findings

- **Strongest case against my conclusion:** the cuts are already visible and moving one way. Managers took one-third of 2023 layoffs (C2), 41% of professionals report manager-level cuts (C3), and spans grew from 10.9 to 12.1 in one year (C5). If spans keep widening at that pace for 5 to 10 years, "most roles gone" is plausible even without AI doing the people work, because fewer managers would each carry far more people (INFERRED). My counter-evidence leans on a 2015 engagement figure (C7), and AI tools for feedback and coaching are improving fast (RECALLED).
- **Against the research:** several sources have a stake. Gartner sells forecasts to executives (C1), Korn Ferry sells leadership services (C3, C8), and Gallup sells manager development (C6, C7). Three figures come through one secondary source (Ramp) because the primary pages would not open (C2, C3, C8). I found no Australian data, and every figure here is global or from US-based firms (INFERRED).
- **Rival explanation for the same facts:** cost pressure after the 2021 to 2022 hiring surge explains the 2023 manager layoffs (C2) at least as well as AI does, and the source itself says the AI link is unproven (C4) (INFERRED).

One line per ledger row:

- C1 holds in part. Holds: as a forecast that a minority of organisations (one in five) will cut deeply. Fails: as support for "most organisations", because the forecast itself limits the cut to one in five. Instead: the evidence points to a split, where some organisations cut hard and most trim layers.
- C2 would be false if the Bloomberg and Live Data Technologies analysis, read in the original, shows a smaller share or a different year than Ramp reports.
- C3 would be false if the Korn Ferry survey, read in the original, measured something narrower than cuts to manager roles.
- C4 unresolved. Would be settled by: layoff or restructure data that separates AI-driven role removal from cost-driven cuts, for example firms that name AI tools as the replacement for specific manager tasks. Not used in the final position.
- C5 would be false if Gallup's 2026 figure falls back towards 10.9, or if the rise comes from a few very large teams while typical teams stay at five to six (Gallup reports the typical manager oversees about five to six people, which already points this way).
- C6 would be false if organisations with wide spans and average managers show engagement and output as good as those with strong managers.
- C7 would be false if newer Gallup or independent studies find the manager's share of engagement variance well below 70%. The 2015 date is the weak point.
- C8 would be false if the Korn Ferry figures describe all employees, not those in organisations that removed layers.
- C9 would be false if flattened organisations show the same rate of internal promotion into leadership as before.
- C10 holds in part. Holds: for scheduling, status reporting and routine monitoring, which a search summary of Gartner's release named (RECALLED). Fails: for feedback, development and judgement calls, where the manager effect in C6 and C7 sits. Instead: AI takes the relay work, and the people work stays with a human manager.
- C11 holds in part. Holds: where roles are mostly relay work, the tasks go and the role goes with them. Fails: where the role is mostly people work, the tasks go and the role changes shape. Instead: in that region, AI frees manager time, and organisations spend it on wider spans (C5), not on zero managers.
- C12 holds in part. Holds: in organisations where most manager hours go on relay and reporting, and in the minority that choose deep flattening (C1). Fails: as a general prediction for most large knowledge-work organisations, because nothing in the ledger shows most organisations cutting most roles, and cuts carry a measured cost (C8, C9). Instead: fewer layers and larger teams (C5), with manager quality mattering more, not less (C6).
- C13 unresolved. Would be settled by: a study that tracks management headcount through the 1990s delayering and after. Not used in the final position.

**Integration.** Two moves.

- **Level shift.** At the task level, AI takes a large share of what middle managers do (C10, RECALLED). At the role level, the job is also the human relationship that drives engagement (C7) and development (C9). Task automation does not reduce to role removal, because removing the tasks leaves the relationship work in place.
- **Conditional.** The claim holds in the region where manager roles are mostly relay and reporting. The observation that places a role in that region: if an AI summary of team status went straight to the next level up, would anyone miss this manager within a month? If no, the role is at risk. If yes, because people lose feedback, direction or development, the role changes shape and stays.

## Adversarial findings
- **Strongest case against the final position:** Bernstein and Turban studied people moving from cubicles in large corporate headquarters. A small team moving from separate rooms or floors into one shared space may gain contact simply because they now see each other at all. The evidence does not rule that case out.
- **Against the research:** Both measured studies come from one research group and two firms. Kim and de Dear rely on survey ratings, not behaviour. I found no measured study showing the opposite, but I searched briefly, so a counter-study could exist.
- **C2 would be false if:** a large post-occupancy survey found open-plan occupants rated ease of interaction no higher than people in private offices.
- **C5 would be false if:** trials of layout changes routinely failed to predict what happened after a permanent move.
- **C1 fails.** Holds: plausibly where the change removes real distance, such as bringing together a team split across rooms or floors (not tested in the sources I retrieved). Fails: where people already sat near each other in cubicles and the walls came down (Bernstein & Turban, 2018). Instead: in that region, face-to-face time fell about 70% and people switched to email and messaging.
- **C3 fails.** Holds: nowhere in the retrieved evidence. Fails: in the longitudinal studies Kim and de Dear cite. Instead: the dissatisfaction persisted, so "give it time" is not a safe line in the proposal.
- **C4 fails.** Holds: removing walls does increase visibility. Fails: visibility did not produce more face-to-face contact. Instead: people used headphones and avoided eye contact to protect focus, so interaction depends on norms and spaces for quiet work as much as on open sight lines.

**Integration (reframe):** Both "open plan increases collaboration" and "open plan kills collaboration" share one hidden assumption: that the layout itself decides how much people talk. C4 removes that assumption. Once it goes, the question becomes which mix of open space, quiet space and team norms gets your team talking about the right work. A trial answers that. The layout alone does not.

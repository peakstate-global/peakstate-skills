# The arc sheet

Fill this in before any slide or paragraph is written, and get it approved.

## Template

**Audience:** who they are, what they already know, what they are responsible for.

**Job to be done:** When [situation], they want to [progress], so they can [outcome].

**Throughline:** one idea, 15 words or fewer, written before the spine.

**Hook and position:** only if the piece opens on a deliberately polarised hook. Write the hook as it stands, then the position the author actually holds, in their plain words: what the hook means, where it overstates, and what the author still believes matters. Every claim stays true to the position. A reviewer's literal reading of the hook is answered by the position, and the argument changes only with the author's approval.

**Where they start:**

| Belief they arrive with | What it costs them | OBSERVED or INFERRED | Section that answers it |
|---|---|---|---|
| … | … | … | … |

**Pains to name:** the frustrations the piece must acknowledge, even where it does not solve them.

**The shift:** from "[what they would say now]" to "[what they can say at the end]".

**The emotional journey:** they arrive feeling [wary, cynical, anxious…] and leave feeling [hopeful, resolved…]. The understanding moment, where their concerns are stated fairly before any promise, is section [n].

**Where to start:** the one action they take afterwards.

**The opening:** the change in the world the room already feels, before any framework.

**The spine:**

| Section | Its premise, in one sentence | Moves them from saying | To saying | They feel | Move, and the line that delivers it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | … | … | … | … | confirm: "I know you're thinking …, and you're right." |

**The moments:**

| Moment | What it is | Section and slide |
|---|---|---|
| Peak | The one emotional high point | … |
| Last 60 seconds | The scripted last line and image, echoing the open | … |
| Open loop | The question planted early, and where it pays off | … and … |
| S.T.A.R. moment | Its type (dramatisation, repeatable line, striking visual, story, shocking statistic made physical, emulated demonstration) and what the room sees | … |
| Credibility story | One specific story, in the first three minutes | … |
| "I was wrong" | What the speaker once believed, and what changed it | … |
| Light moments | Each one, the first straight after the heaviest slide | … |
| Stories | One per section, from the story bank, or marked as a gap | … |
| Statistics | Each one, with its person or physical scale | … |

Rules for the spine:

- The "to saying" of each section is the "from saying" of the next.
- Vocabulary comes before application: teach the options before the audience applies them to its own case.
- The last section answers "where do I start?", not "what is the method?".
- Read the premises alone, top to bottom. If they do not make the argument, the spine is not finished.
- Read the feelings alone, top to bottom. If the room is asked to hope before it feels understood, move the understanding section earlier.
- Read the moves alone, top to bottom. A confirm or a justify comes before the first promise, and all five moves appear.
- Every section serves the throughline. Name any that serves it only indirectly.

## Worked example: a 90 minute workshop on where AI belongs in the work

**Audience:** internal agile and product coaches in a large organisation. They use a desktop assistant, hear "agents" everywhere, and are asked by teams and executives which work AI should take.

**Job to be done:** When a team or executive asks what to automate, they want a way to choose, so they can give an answer they can defend and start somewhere safe.

**Throughline:** "Choose the work first; most AI ideas should die cheaply in the room." (13 words)

**Where they start:**

| Belief they arrive with | What it costs them | Label | Section |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI means a chatbot | They miss the plays already running, such as scoring models | INFERRED | 2 |
| The question is which jobs AI replaces | Roles are bundles of work, so the question has no answer | OBSERVED | 3 |
| Agents are the destination | Ideas go to the most expensive, least predictable play, when a workflow often returns more | OBSERVED | 2 and 6 |
| We have to be seen doing AI | Pilot sprawl, pilots that return nothing, and fatigue | INFERRED | 5 |
| A human in the loop makes it safe | The person at the gate cannot keep up, so it becomes a rubber stamp or a chokepoint | OBSERVED | 6 |

**The shift:** from "AI is a chatbot, and we should find processes it can take over" to "AI is four different plays, we choose the work first, kill most ideas cheaply, and design what the human at the gate is for".

**Where to start:** one bounded, checkable workflow step that repeats every week, with a named person at the gate.

**The opening:** teams across the organisation already use AI every day, and nobody chose which work it should take.

**The spine:**

| Section | Premise | From saying | To saying | Move |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Open | You hold beliefs about AI that are costing you | "This is one more AI session." | "I recognise one of these beliefs." | confirm: "I know you're thinking this is one more AI session, and you're right to be tired of them." |
| 2. What AI is now | AI is four plays that differ in who holds the loop, cost and return | "Agents are where it is heading." | "The most autonomous play is the most expensive, not the most valuable." | justify: "Your pilots stalled because they started at the most expensive play, not because your teams got it wrong." |
| 3. Which work | The unit is the work, never the job | "Which jobs will AI replace?" | "I can place my own work and name its posture." | encourage: "You can be the person who answers 'what do we automate?' with a reason." |
| 4. Match | Each posture reaches for different plays | "Any AI will do." | "I know which plays fit my work." | on your side: "We'll place my own work first, so you can see me get it wrong." |
| 5. Kill it cheaply | Most candidates should die in the room | "We should start a pilot." | "My candidate survives five questions, or I saved a quarter." | allay: "Killing an idea in the room is not failure. Here are the five questions that do it in ten minutes." |
| 6. Price it honestly | The person at the gate sets the ceiling | "A human in the loop makes it safe." | "I know what my gate's person is for, and whether they can keep up." | allay: "The gate is where the risk sits, and you can size it before you build." |
| 7. Start here | Start one rung lower, with a named gate and a date | "Where do I start?" | "I leave with one candidate, its play, its gate and a date." | on your side: "Pick one; I'll take the first call on it with you." |

**The moments:** the peak is section 5, where the room's own candidates die on a show of hands (a live scoring beat, the S.T.A.R. moment of type dramatisation). The open loop is a question in section 1, "which of your ideas would you bet a quarter on?", paid off in section 7. The credibility story, in the first three minutes, is the facilitator's own pilot that ran a quarter and returned nothing. The "I was wrong" beat sits in section 2: the facilitator once argued agents were the destination. The first light moment follows section 5's kills. The close mirrors the title slide and repeats the throughline.

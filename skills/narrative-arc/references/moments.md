# The moments

Read this when choosing a move and its line, or planning the peak, the S.T.A.R. moment, the stories, the confession or the light moments. The arc sheet records each choice (`references/arc-sheet.md`). `/draft-eval`'s moments group checks the built deck against the same list.

## The five moves

Every section, and every presented content slide, makes one move. The speaker note opens with the line that delivers it. Title and evidence slides may carry none. Warren's sentence names the first four (S3); the fifth replaces his "help them throw rocks at their enemies".

| Move | Tag | What the slide does | A line for a room of leaders |
|---|---|---|---|
| Encourage their dreams | `encourage` | Names the future the room already wants, before any problem | "You already know the version of this organisation that ships what customers ask for." |
| Justify their failures | `justify` | Gives a past stall a structural cause, so the room is not to blame | "The programme didn't stall because of the people in this room. It optimised the thing that was scarce twenty years ago." |
| Allay their fears | `allay` | Names the quiet risk, then the first step that handles it | "Yes, this changes your security exposure. Here is the gate that handles it, and you can add it on Monday." |
| Confirm their suspicions | `confirm` | Says out loud what half the room privately believes, and agrees | "I know you're thinking the ceremonies became theatre, and you're right." |
| Always on your side | `on your side` | The speaker stands with the room: "we", a shared problem, the speaker's own stake in their success | "We go into this together. I have made every one of these mistakes, and I want you to skip them." |

Order the moves so a confirm or a justify comes before the first promise. A promise is a slide that offers a better future, a fix or the piece's answer. A promise heard before the room feels understood sounds like a pitch. All five moves appear across the piece; the same move twice in a row is fine when the content calls for it.

"Confirm their suspicions" works because agreeing with what someone already believes lowers the guard that blocks the next, harder idea. Say it as agreement ("and you're right"), never as an instruction ("you should think").

## Throughline and opening

- **The throughline** is one idea in 15 words or fewer (S4). Every section serves it. A section that serves it only indirectly is either cut, or named out loud, often in the confession.
- **Open on the change in the world**: what is already moving around the room, which the audience feels but has not named. The framework comes after the room agrees the world has changed.

## The peak and the end

People judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and its ending, and largely ignore its length (the peak-end rule). So:

- **Name one emotional peak.** It is usually the S.T.A.R. moment or the confession. Spend most of the design effort on the peak and on the last 60 seconds; the middle may be plain.
- **Script the last 60 seconds** word for word: the last line, the last image, and where to start.
- **The close returns to the open.** The last slide mirrors the title slide's layout, and its line echoes the opening line or the throughline, so the room feels the piece was about one thing.
- **One open loop.** Ask a question early that the room cannot yet answer, build the frame that makes the answer possible, then pay it off near the end. A question pulls only once the room knows enough to feel the gap.

## The S.T.A.R. moment

One moment per piece that the room repeats afterwards (S5). Name its type and the slide it lives on.

| Type | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Dramatisation | The room feels the problem before it is explained: a prop, a live act |
| Repeatable line | One sentence written to be said in the corridor afterwards |
| Striking visual | One slide unlike every other slide in the piece |
| Story | A short personal story that turns on one five-second moment |
| Shocking statistic made physical | A number shown as something the room can see or hold (S7) |
| Emulated demonstration | A live demonstration replayed on slides, so nothing can fail on the night |

Pause after it. Let the room answer its own question before you speak.

## Stories and people

- **The story bank first.** The story bank is a file of the author's own stories, each tagged by topic, by move and by the five-second moment it turns on (S6). For each section, pull a story from the bank before inventing an example. If the bank has no story for a section, say so on the arc sheet as a gap, and ask the author for one.
- **Every statistic gets a person or a physical scale.** A figure stands beside one person it happened to, or is shown at a scale the room can picture: a barrow of sugar, a stack of paper (S7).

## Credibility and confession

- **Credibility in the first three minutes, through a story.** One specific moment that shows why the speaker has the right to say this. Never a CV, a list of titles or a wall of logos.
- **One "I was wrong" beat.** A time the speaker believed what the piece now argues against, or a failure of their own. Vulnerability gives the room permission to change its own mind (S8). Place it near the peak, often straight after the slide that challenges the room most.
- **The confession serves the argument.** What the speaker was wrong about is the belief the piece now corrects, so the beat carries the room toward the throughline. It never concedes a counter-argument: a confession that grants the other side's case comes at the moment the room trusts the speaker most, so it does the most damage to the piece. If an objection deserves a place, answer it in its own section, not in the confession.
- **Reviewers raise, the author decides.** An objection from a reviewer, a grader or a model never changes the argument or the narrative on its own, the confession included. Raise it with the author with what it would change, and change the piece only on the author's explicit approval, because the author is the one who has to stand behind it.

## Light moments

- **At least one, planned.** A joke, a self-deprecating line, a playful image or a comic callback, written into the notes at a point you can name.
- **Straight after the heaviest slide**, where the room most needs release. Self-deprecating humour works best: the joke is on the speaker, never on the room.
- **More is better.** Playfulness buys permission to stay with dry material (S9).

This skill designs the arc of a deck or article first, so every section earns its place by moving the audience one step from where they are to where the piece should leave them.

## Steps

The topic, any draft and any notes on the audience are the input. If they hold names, customer data, staff or other sensitive detail, say once: "Use only a tool your organisation has approved for this information."

1. **Name the audience.** Who is in the room or reading, what they already know, and what they are responsible for. One audience per piece. If there are two, ask which one the piece is for, and name the other as out of scope.
2. **Name the job to be done.** The job the audience hires this piece to do: the progress they want to make in their own work, not the topic (S2). Write it as "When [situation], they want to [progress], so they can [outcome]."
3. **Write the throughline.** One idea, in 15 words or fewer, that the whole piece serves (S4). Write it before the spine. Every section must serve it; name any section that serves it only indirectly, and cut or justify it. The throughline also tells you what to cut.
   - **Hook and position.** A piece may open on a deliberately polarised hook, such as a title that overstates the case to start a conversation. If it does, write the position the author actually holds beside the hook in the arc sheet, in the author's own plain words: what they mean, where the hook overstates, and what they still believe matters. Every claim in the piece must stay true to the position, even where the hook alone would allow more. The hook stays as written.
4. **Map where they start.** List the beliefs they arrive with, the pains they feel and the common misunderstandings, then write beside each belief what it costs them. Keep only beliefs that send effort in the wrong direction. Label each one OBSERVED (the user or a source says so) or INFERRED (your reasoning). Ask the user to confirm any INFERRED belief that the arc leans on, because a wrong belief costs its section the hook.
5. **Name the shift.** One sentence: from what they would say now to what they can say at the end. The audience is the hero and the piece is the mentor (S1). Then say the one action they take after it: where to start. The piece opens on the change in the world that the audience already feels, before any framework or method.
6. **Map the emotional journey.** Beliefs change only once the audience feels understood, so plan the feelings as well as the arguments. Name how they feel arriving (wary, cynical, anxious, bored, quietly afraid) and how they should feel leaving. Then plan three moves:
   - **Understanding comes first.** Before any promise, a section states their concerns in their own words, better than they would put them, and agrees with what is true in each (S3, "confirm their suspicions"). A promise made before that moment sounds like a sales pitch, and the room stops listening.
   - **Contrast what is with what could be.** Move back and forth between the reality they know and the better state the piece offers, so the gap pulls them forward (S1).
   - **Earn the close.** End on hope or resolve they have reached themselves. A dream at the start is believed only if the audience can see you do not believe the brochure version either; if the piece opens on one, open on the brochure version and close on the earned one.
   Each section, and later each slide, makes one of the five moves, chosen on purpose, with the line that delivers it (S3, with the fifth move changed): **encourage their dreams**, **justify their failures**, **allay their fears**, **confirm their suspicions** ("I know you're thinking X, and you're right"), and **always on your side** (we go into battle together). Never tell an audience not to be afraid. What each move does, and a line for each, are in `references/moments.md`.
7. **Plan the moments.** Decide what the room will remember before any slide exists, and write each one into the arc sheet. The detail and examples are in `references/moments.md`.
   - **The moves.** Every section and slide names its move and its line. A confirm or a justify comes before the first promise (a slide that offers a better future, a fix or the piece's answer). All five moves appear.
   - **The peak and the end.** Name the one emotional peak and script the last 60 seconds; most design effort goes there, and the middle may be plain. The close returns to the open: the last line or image echoes the first. Plant one open loop early (a question the room cannot yet answer) and pay it off late.
   - **One S.T.A.R. moment**, Something They'll Always Remember (S5). Name its type (a dramatisation, a repeatable line, a striking visual, a story, a shocking statistic made physical, or an emulated demonstration) and the section and slide it lives on.
   - **Stories and people.** Pull each section's story from the author's story bank before inventing an example (S6). Every statistic gets a person it happened to or a physical scale the room can picture (S7).
   - **Credibility and confession.** In the first three minutes, one specific story shows why the speaker has the right to say this, never a CV. One "I was wrong" beat: a time the speaker believed what the piece now argues against (S8). The confession serves the argument: it moves the room toward the throughline and never concedes a counter-argument, because a concession at the most trusted moment of the piece undoes the case it makes.
   - **Light moments.** Plan at least one, self-deprecating, straight after the heaviest slide, where the room most needs release (S9). More is better.
8. **Build the spine.** Order the sections so each one corrects one starting belief or builds a capability the next section needs. For every section write six things: its link from the section before (**but**, a turn, or **therefore**, a consequence) (S10), its premise in one sentence, what the audience says on the way in, what they can say on the way out, what they feel by the end of it, and its move with the line that delivers it. The way out of one section is the way in to the next. Teach the vocabulary before you apply it. End on where to start, not on a method. Then, before any building, name the shape of each section's idea (a journey is a map, a spread is a curve, a filter is a funnel, a ladder shows every rung) so the build draws that shape, and plan one device per slide, varied across the piece: a guess before a reveal, a surprise, a callback, a live challenge, or curiosity. Use the template in `references/arc-sheet.md`.
9. **Test the spine.** Read only the premises, top to bottom: they must read as the argument of the whole piece. Read them again with each link between them: every join is a but or a therefore, and an "and then" join marks a section that lists instead of argues, so give it a cause or merge it. A run of parallel items counts as one beat; the rules are in `references/arc-sheet.md`. Then read only the feelings, top to bottom: they must read as a journey with no jump the room cannot make, and the understanding moment must come before the first promise. Then read only the moves: a confirm or a justify comes before the first promise, and all five appear. Check that the throughline is served by every section, that the peak, the last 60 seconds, the S.T.A.R. moment, the open loop and its payoff, the credibility story, the "I was wrong" beat and a light moment are each placed, and that every starting belief is answered by a named section, that no section moves nobody, and that each idea is modelled once: two sections that model the same idea with different axes or terms get merged. Cut or merge any section that fails.
10. **Write the terms sheet.** List every term the piece will use that is not plain English, and decide for each one: keep it, or replace it with plain words. Keep a term only if it earns its place. For each kept term, write what it means here and where the piece introduces it: early, visually, with a metaphor or an example, before its first use. An id such as D1 is fine when its letter means something (A for AI) and the id is reused wherever that item appears; each framework gets one look, used everywhere and unlike every other framework's. Once introduced, a term may be reused later; where it returns, let context clues imply its meaning. List the replaced terms too, each with the plain words used instead. In a deck, the sheet becomes the hidden terms slide (the peakstate-deck skill says how).
11. **Get the spine and the terms sheet approved before building.** Show the arc sheet and the terms sheet, and ask for sign-off. Nothing is drafted until both are agreed.
12. **Build to the spine.** For a deck, the slides carry the claims, and the speaker notes carry the narrative: each note opens with the line that delivers the slide's move, says what its slide claims, and gives the line that carries the audience from the last slide into this one and on to the next, carrying the section's link (a but or a therefore) in its sense. The notes carry stage directions in square brackets ([MOVE: x], [PAUSE], [ASK], [STORY], [LIGHT]); the peakstate-deck skill says how to build each moment and mark it. The first slide of each section names the belief it answers. For an article, the headings state each section's premise, and the first sentence of each section links from the last one. Only add text that adds value.
13. **Score the draft.** `/draft-eval` is the bar for a draft: it scores every section against the rubric before anyone reviews it. For a deck, its moments group is the check on this skill's moments, and every check in it must pass: `star_moment`, `peak_and_end`, `five_moves`, `people_and_stories`, `wrong_and_credible`, `light_moment` and `stage_directions`.

    **The argument belongs to the author.** An objection from a reviewer, a grader or a model never changes the piece's argument, throughline or narrative without the author's explicit approval. Raise the objection with the author, say what it would change, and wait. The author has to stand behind the argument in the room, and a reviewer sees only one draft of it.

    **A literal reading of the hook is answered by the position.** When a reviewer objects to what a polarised hook says on its own, answer with the position recorded in the arc sheet, and clarify the piece so it carries the position (a definition, a footnote, a line in the notes). Never change the argument, and never add a concession, without the author's approval.

## The take-away

The arc sheet: the audience, the job to be done, the starting map (belief, what it costs, OBSERVED or INFERRED, and the section that answers it), the throughline, the hook and the position behind it (if the piece opens on a polarised hook), the shift in one sentence, the emotional journey (arriving, leaving, and where the understanding moment sits), where to start, the spine table (section, premise, from, to, feel, move and its line), and the moments (the peak, the last 60 seconds, the open loop, the S.T.A.R. moment, the credibility story, the "I was wrong" beat, the light moments and each section's story). The template and a worked example are in `references/arc-sheet.md`.

## Next

Your next three moves:

- Confirm or correct each INFERRED belief.
- Read the premises alone, top to bottom, and say whether they make the argument.
- Check that the S.T.A.R. moment and the "I was wrong" beat are true stories you can tell.
- Approve the spine and the moments, then build to them.

## Self-check before you deliver

- The audience is one named group, and the job to be done is written as progress they want, not as the topic.
- The throughline is one idea in 15 words or fewer, written before the spine, and the piece opens on the change in the world before any framework. A polarised hook has the author's position written beside it, and no claim goes past that position.
- Every starting belief has its cost and its label, and is answered by a named section.
- Every section has a premise, a from, a to, a move and its line; each to is the next from; each join is a but or a therefore; each idea has one model and a named shape; the devices vary.
- Read alone, the premises make the argument, the feelings make a journey, and the moves put a confirm or a justify before the first promise, with all five appearing.
- The moments are placed: the peak, the last 60 seconds echoing the open, an open loop paid off late, one S.T.A.R. moment with its type and slide, a credibility story in the first three minutes, one "I was wrong" beat, and a light moment after the heaviest slide.
- Each section has a story or a named person, from the story bank first, and no statistic stands without a person or a physical scale.
- The terms sheet lists every term that is not plain English and where each kept term is introduced; the spine ends on where to start; nothing is drafted before the user approves the spine; in the built piece, each deck note opens with its move's line and carries its stage directions.
